Scaling Claims Without Scaling Headcount

How I put an AI agent inside every claim at Pillow and gave my handlers a quarter of their time back.

By Marcel Beno, COO, Pillow

Why I am writing this

I am Marcel Beno, COO of Pillow, a fast-growing challenger insurer. A year ago I was facing the classic operations problem: my claims volume was growing faster than I could sensibly hire. Today my claim handlers spend 20–25% less time per case, and I did not do it by adding a single extra shift. I put an always-on AI agent inside every claim.

The agent has one job: to check that every document arriving in a claim is complete, correct and consistent with everything else in the file. It runs 24/7, reacts the moment a document arrives, and keeps a live health check on each claim. My people no longer spend their days cross-checking paperwork. They spend them on the cases that need judgment, negotiation and empathy.

I kept the technical side deliberately simple. We kept our standard core claims system and call ClaimSense as an API from inside it. No platform migration, no parallel tool. ClaimSense lets me pick the AI models I want and tune the agent's behaviour separately for each product line, from MTPL (motor third-party liability) to Casco (cover for damage to your own vehicle).

This is the story of how I did it, told plainly, so that you can do the same.

The problem on my desk

We had just brought claims handling in-house after years with an external partner. I pushed for that decision and I would make it again. Insourcing gave us control over the client experience, the data and the speed of every decision. It also meant that the claims team was now my cost, my bottleneck and my responsibility.

At the same time, Pillow was doing what a challenger is supposed to do: growing fast. Every policy we sold was a future claim I would have to handle. The maths kept me up at night. If claims grew 40% a year, did my claims team have to grow 40% a year too?

I saw three options:

  • Hire more and more people.Predictable, but slow, expensive and hard to unwind. Good claim handlers are scarce, and every new hire needs months before they are truly productive.
  • Let service slip.Cheap in the short term and fatal for a challenger whose whole pitch is being better than the incumbents.
  • Think differently.Look at what my handlers actually do all day, find the part that does not need a human, and hand that part to a machine.

So I went and sat with the team. What I saw was that a large share of every handler's day was not decision-making at all. It was checking. Did the client send the police report? Does the registration number match across the claim form, the photos and the repair estimate? Is the invoice date consistent with the incident date? What is still missing before this case can move?

A large share of every handler's day was not decision-making at all. It was checking.

This work matters. It is also repetitive, rule-based and constantly interrupting. Documents arrive at random times, and every arrival means someone opens the file, re-reads it and re-checks it. I realised this was exactly the kind of work an AI agent could do better than any of us, and it was the obvious place to start.

What I decided

I decided to hand one clearly bounded part of the claims process to AI agents: the factual completeness and cross-validation of client documents. Everything else, from liability assessment to reserving and settlement, stayed with my people.

I set myself three rules:

  1. Start with the boring part.My first AI use case had to be something my handlers were glad to give up, not something they felt threatened by. Document checking was the clear winner. Nobody joined Pillow to compare registration numbers across PDFs.
  2. Do not touch the core system.Our standard claims system holds the process, the audit trail and all our integrations. Ripping it out to add AI would have turned a quarter's project into a multi-year programme. The AI had to plug into what we already had.
  3. Put the agent inside the claim, not beside it.I did not want another tool my team had to remember to open. The agent runs continuously inside each claim case and pushes its findings to the screen my handlers already work in.

With those rules, the technology choice was quick. I chose ClaimSense, an AI claims solution built to be called as an API from a core claims system. It let me deploy an agent per claim, leave our core system untouched, and adjust the agent's behaviour per product line without writing new code for each one.

How it works today

Every claim case in our core system now has its own AI agent, running from the moment the claim is opened until it is closed. The agent has one continuous question in mind: is this claim factually complete and internally consistent right now?

Reading the flow: the client sends a document, our core system passes it to ClaimSense, ClaimSense re-checks the whole claim and returns an updated health status, and my handler only steps in when something needs a human.

What the agent checks on every document arrival

  • Completeness.Are all documents required for this claim type and product line present? If not, exactly which ones are missing?
  • Correctness.Are the extracted facts plausible? Dates in the right order, amounts within expected ranges, mandatory fields filled?
  • Cross-validation.Do the documents agree with each other and with the claim form? Same vehicle, same people, same incident date, same location, same damage description across the police report, photos, invoices and estimates.

What my handlers see

Inside the claim screen they already know, a live health check: green when the file is complete and consistent, amber with a plain-language list of gaps or contradictions when it is not. Nobody has to re-read the file to find out what changed. The agent already did that, at 3 a.m. if that is when the document came in.

Why 24/7 was the whole point for me

Documents arrive whenever clients, garages and police stations send them. Before, each arrival sat in a queue until a handler had time to open the case, re-orient and re-check. Now the check is done by the time my team starts the day. The gap between "document received" and "claim ready for the next step" has collapsed, and a handler's first action on a case is a decision, not a review.

The integration

Our core claims system calls ClaimSense over a standard API. When a document lands in a claim, the core system sends it to ClaimSense together with the claim context. ClaimSense returns structured findings that the core system stores and displays. No handler logs into a second tool, no data is copied by hand, and our core system remains the single source of truth.

Where the freed time went

I did not use the 20–25% to cut headcount. I used it to absorb growth and to raise quality. My handlers now spend more time on:

  • Complex and contested claims, where experience and negotiation skill change the outcome.
  • Direct contact with clients at the moments that matter most, instead of chasing paperwork.
  • Suspected fraud and edge cases, where a flagged contradiction needs a person to investigate.

What it meant for my growth question

My claims team now handles more claims per person. The choice I thought I had to make, hire at the pace of growth or let service slip, turned out to be a false one. The team still grows, but at a fraction of the rate of the claims volume, and the people who join spend their first weeks learning to decide, not to check.

The point was never to remove people from claims. It was to stop wasting a single second of their time on something a machine does better.

Why it was easier than I expected: ClaimSense as an API

The whole project stayed small because ClaimSense fits into our core claims system rather than replacing it. Three things made the difference for me.

1. It is just an API call from our core system

Our standard claims system already handles document intake, workflow and the audit trail. Connecting ClaimSense meant adding one outbound call when a document arrives and one place to display the returned findings. My claims team kept its screens, its process and its data. My IT team kept its architecture.

2. I choose the models

ClaimSense is not locked to a single AI model. I can select which underlying models the agent uses and change that choice over time as models improve, prices move or compliance requirements shift. As a European insurer, I want control over where our data goes and which vendors we depend on. This gave me that control.

3. I tune the behaviour per product line

An MTPL claim and a Casco claim do not need the same checks. Neither does a property claim or a travel claim. ClaimSense lets me configure the agent's behaviour for each claims process separately:

If you want to do the same

Nothing in my setup is exotic, and that is the point. If you run claims at an insurer with a core system and an IT team that can call an API, you can follow the same path. Here is what I would tell you to do.

  1. Measure the checking first.Sit with your handlers for a week and log how much time goes into document completeness and cross-checking versus actual decisions. For me it was the 20–25% that later disappeared.
  2. Pick one product line.Start with the line that has the highest volume and the most standardised document set. I started with MTPL.
  3. Write down the document set and the rules.For that line, define which documents are required, which facts must agree across them, and what counts as a contradiction. This becomes the agent's configuration in ClaimSense.
  4. Wire the API.Add a call from your core claims system to ClaimSense on every document arrival, and a place in the claim screen to show the health check. Keep your core system as the source of truth.
  5. Run it in shadow mode.Let the agent check claims for a few weeks while your handlers still do their own checks. Compare. Tune the rules and thresholds until your team trusts the findings.
  6. Switch your handlers off checking.Once the trust is there, make the health check the starting point of every case. Redirect the freed time explicitly to complex claims and client contact, and tell your team that is the plan.
  7. Add the next product line.Casco, property, travel: each one is a new configuration on the same integration.

What I learned along the way

  • Pick a task people are happy to give away. Adoption was never an issue for me because nobody missed the checking.
  • Do not build a parallel system. Because the agent lives inside the claim, it is used on every case by default.
  • Configure per product line from day one. One generic checker would have been too strict for some lines and too loose for others.
  • Keep model choice in your own hands. Being able to swap models later removed a whole category of vendor-risk objections from my board conversations.
  • Measure before and after. A concrete 20–25% figure did more for my business case than any slide about AI.

A closing word

I did not innovate Pillow's claims operation by buying a big platform or launching a transformation programme. I found the one piece of work my people were quietly losing a quarter of their day to, and I gave it to an agent that never sleeps. Everything else stayed the same, which is exactly why it worked.

If you are facing the same choice I was, hire more people or think differently, I hope this account helps you choose the second one. I am happy to compare notes with any operations leader who wants to try it.
